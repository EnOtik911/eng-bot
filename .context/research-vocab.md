## 1. Core spoken English coverage

### 1.1 Coverage of frequent word families — the numbers, and where they disagree

**Nation (2006), "How Large a Vocabulary Is Needed for Reading and Listening?"** (https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf):
- 3,000 word families (with proper nouns included) → **95% coverage of unscripted spoken English**.
- 6,000–9,000 word families → **98% coverage of spoken English**.
- For written text: ~8,000–9,000 word families needed for 98% coverage (unassisted reading of novels).

**Adolphs & Schmitt (2003), "Lexical Coverage of Spoken Discourse"**, *Applied Linguistics* 24(4) (https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/24/4/425/213596, https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ678005), analysing the CANCODE spoken corpus:
- 2,000 word families cover **less than 95%** of spoken discourse in this corpus — a materially more pessimistic figure than the older assumption that 2,000 words is "enough" for speech.
- Approximately **5,000 word families needed for ~96% coverage** of the CANCODE spoken data.
- This is a direct empirical correction to earlier, looser claims (see 1.2 below) that 2,000 words suffices for conversational coverage.

**van Zeeland & Schmitt (2012)**, cited via replication study Cobb et al., *Language Teaching* 2017 (https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1139942, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE):
- Argues **95% coverage** (not 98%) is the more realistic minimum threshold for **listening** comprehension, because comprehension scores show less variance improvement between 95%→98% than between 90%→95%.
- Estimates 95% coverage of spoken text is reachable with **2,000–3,000 word families**.
- **This partially contradicts Adolphs & Schmitt's 2003 CANCODE finding** that 2,000 families give <95%. The two studies used different corpora and methods; both are peer-reviewed and neither supersedes the other. **Contested — flag explicitly**: the "2,000–3,000 words is enough for spoken comprehension" claim is defensible per van Zeeland & Schmitt but directly disputed by Adolphs & Schmitt's corpus analysis, which finds you need closer to 5,000.

**Written vs. spoken coverage differential** (reported via the ERIC EJ1139942 replication study and related SAGE literature, exact primary attribution unclear from available excerpts — flagged as lower-confidence figure): at the same frequency band, written text is consistently better covered than spoken text — e.g., approximate figures found: 1,000 families → ~98% written vs. ~92% spoken; 2,000 → ~95% written vs. ~90% spoken; 3,000 → ~95% written vs. ~87% spoken. **Caveat: I could not trace this specific table to one named primary paper with full confidence — treat as directionally correct (written > spoken coverage at every band) but do not cite the exact percentages as settled.**

**The widely-repeated popular claim "2,000 words = 80% of everyday conversation"**: found repeated across non-academic vocabulary/language-learning blogs (migaku.com, dailyenglish, universeofmemory.com) with **no clear primary citation** traceable from these sources. The closest defensible academic anchor, cited secondhand, is Hwang (1989), Hirsh & Nation (1992), and Sutarsyah, Nation & Kennedy (1994), which are reported (via Nation's own later work) as supporting **"1,000 words → ~80% of non-specialised language."** **No primary-source verification obtained for the specific "2,000 words / 80% conversation" figure — treat it as a popularised approximation, not as reliable as the Nation/Adolphs-Schmitt/van Zeeland figures above.**

### 1.2 Vocabulary size threshold: conversational fluency vs. reading comprehension

These are genuinely different numbers depending on what "threshold" means — coverage-for-comprehension vs. size-for-productive-fluency are different constructs, and the field is not fully settled on the second:

| Purpose | Word families needed | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 95% coverage of spoken text (van Zeeland & Schmitt's proposed minimum) | 2,000–3,000 | van Zeeland & Schmitt 2012, via Cobb et al. replication |
| 95% coverage of spoken text (Adolphs & Schmitt's stricter CANCODE estimate) | ~5,000 | Adolphs & Schmitt 2003 |
| Unassisted comprehension of spoken text (98% coverage) | 6,000–7,000 | Nation 2006 |
| 95% coverage for independent extensive reading | ~3,000 | Nation 2006 |
| Unassisted comprehension of written text (98% coverage) | 8,000–9,000 | Nation 2006 |
| "Genuine fluency" (loosely defined, receptive) | 8,000–10,000 | Low-confidence, non-academic aggregator sources (vocabulary-test.com, mikeydoes.com) — **flagged as weak sourcing, do not treat as a hard target** |

**Bottom line, stated plainly**: there is no single agreed number for "functional conversational fluency." What the peer-reviewed literature agrees on is that **spoken comprehension at a genuinely comfortable level (95–98% coverage) requires somewhere between 2,000 and 7,000 word families depending on which study and which coverage threshold you accept**, and that this is **lower** than what is needed for unassisted reading of general written text (8,000–9,000). For a learner starting near-zero spoken output, the actionable takeaway is: 2,000–3,000 word families is a defensible first major milestone (contested lower bound), 5,000–6,000 is the more conservative, better-evidenced target for comfortable spoken comprehension.

### 1.3 Multi-word units, collocations, formulaic language — do they matter more than single words for perceived fluency?

**Yes — this is well-evidenced, with actual numbers:**

- **Erman & Warren (2000)**, "The Idiom Principle and the Open-Choice Principle" (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/262289139_The_Idiom_Principle_and_the_Open_Choice_Principle): manual analysis of 19 extracts found **formulaic sequences ("prefabs") make up 58.6% of spoken text and 52.3% of written text**. This is the most frequently cited quantitative estimate of how much of natural discourse is prefabricated rather than freely generated word-by-word.

- **Boers, Eyckmans, Kappel, Stengers & Demecheleer (2006)**, "Formulaic sequences and perceived oral proficiency: putting a Lexical Approach to the test", *Language Teaching Research* (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1191/1362168806lr195oa): experimentally taught one group to notice formulaic sequences (22 hours instruction, N=32 total). Blind native-speaker judges rated the experimental group as more proficient; independent judges' counts of formulaic sequences used in interviews **correlated moderately with oral-proficiency ratings (rho ≈ .438–.446)**. This is direct empirical support that **using more multi-word units is associated with being judged more fluent**, independent of raw vocabulary size.

- General mechanism (Wray's formulaic-language theory, Ellis's chunking/automatization research, described consistently across the sources reviewed): multi-word units are retrieved as single processing units, which **reduces real-time cognitive load during production**, freeing attention for content and reducing hesitation — the standard explanation for why chunk-learners sound more fluent even at equal single-word vocabulary size.

**Conclusion for Q1.3**: the evidence supports building the SRS card unit around collocations/chunks rather than isolated words — which matches the diagnosis already recorded in this project's PROJECT.md ("the card unit is therefore a collocation, not a word"). This diagnosis is now backed by external evidence, not just internal reasoning.

## 2. What professional vocabulary actually matters

### 2.1 Business Service List (BSL) — Browne & Culligan

Source: https://www.newgeneralservicelist.com/business-service-list (New General Service List Project, official page)
- **1,700 words**, built from a **64-million-word corpus of business English texts**.
- Combined with the NGSL (see below), gives **up to 97% coverage of general business texts**.
- Citation: Browne, C., Culligan, B. (2013/2015 — the project page uses both years inconsistently across pages; primary citation is Browne & Culligan 2013 via newgeneralservicelist.com).
- **No isolated BSL-alone coverage percentage was found** in the sources reviewed — only the combined NGSL+BSL figure (97%) is documented. Flagging this as a gap: I could not find "BSL alone covers X% of business text" as a standalone published number.

### 2.2 New General Service List (NGSL) — the general-English baseline this sits on top of

Source: https://www.eapfoundation.com/vocab/general/ngsl/, https://www.newgeneralservicelist.com/new-general-service-list, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_General_Service_List
- **2,801 words**, built from a 273-million-word subsection of a corpus (vs. the original 1953 West GSL's 2.5-million-word corpus — two orders of magnitude larger).
- Reported coverage: **~92% average coverage of general English texts**; higher in specific genres (93% for a Harry Potter novel, 94% for TOEIC exam text, 95% for TV show transcripts like *Friends*, per eapfoundation.com's summary).

### 2.3 Original General Service List (West, 1953) — for comparison, and the "coverage gain" question directly

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Service_List
- ~2,000 words. Reported to give **90–95% coverage of colloquial speech and 80–85% coverage of common written text** for someone who knows the list plus its word-family derivatives.
- **Coverage gain from a specialised list on top of general 2000**: the clearest documented figure is the **Academic Word List (AWL)**, not a business or IT list specifically (see 2.4) — AWL adds **~10 percentage points of coverage on top of the GSL's ~80% for academic text specifically** (i.e., GSL 2000 + AWL 570 ≈ 90% coverage of academic text, per eapfoundation.com and the AWL's own documentation). For business English specifically, the documented gain is the BSL+NGSL combined 97% figure above, but I could not find a clean "NGSL alone gives X%, +BSL gives Y%" delta stated in one place — the 97% is reported only as the combined figure.

### 2.4 Academic Word List (AWL) — Coxhead (2000)

Source: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.2307/3587951 (original TESOL Quarterly paper), https://www.eapfoundation.com/vocab/academic/awllists/
- **570 word families**, compiled from a 3.5-million-word corpus of written academic text, selected as words occurring outside the first 2,000 most frequent general words.
- Coverage: **~10.0% of tokens in academic text**, but only **~1.4% of tokens in a same-size fiction collection** — evidence that the list is genuinely academic-register-specific, not general vocabulary in disguise.
- Relevance to this project: AWL is NOT business/IT-specific, but its "general 2000 + register-specific top-up list" pattern is exactly the structure the BSL and IT word lists below replicate — useful as the general precedent that specialised lists are a real, evidence-backed technique, not just a rule-of-thumb.

### 2.5 IT / Computer Science specific word lists

Several exist, all smaller and less standardised than AWL/BSL. Found via search (not independently verified in full text):
- **"A New Computer Science Academic Word List" (NCSAWL)** — corpus of 2,500,990 running words from 300 CS research articles; resulting list has **444 words, ~20.33% coverage of the CS research-article corpus**. (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369877950_A_New_Computer_Science_Academic_Word_List)
- A second, larger study built from 10,450,035 running words across 1,045 CS research articles, extracting **1,262 word types** by range/frequency criteria (source title: "Developing a Technical Words List for Research Articles in Computer Science Discipline", https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1230952.pdf).
- **Computer Science Academic Vocabulary List (CSAVL)** — built from a 3.5-million-word corpus of CS textbooks/journals, explicitly positioned as a pedagogical ESP resource (source: pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu, Roesler dissertation).

**Important gap, stated explicitly**: none of these IT word lists are aimed at business analysis / systems analysis vocabulary, and none are aimed at fintech/payments. They are aimed at **academic CS research-paper English** (algorithms, computational complexity, formal methods), which is a different register from what an IT Business Analyst uses day to day (requirements, stakeholders, user stories, APIs, payment flows). **No evidence found of a published word list specific to "IT Business Analyst" or "Systems Analyst" register** — this vocabulary has to be assembled from the domain-specific glossaries in sections 3 and 4 below, not from a corpus word-frequency list.

### 3.3 Agile / product-delivery terminology a systems analyst uses daily

Sources: Scrum Alliance glossary (https://www.scrumalliance.org/glossary), Scrum.org glossary (https://www.scrum.org/resources/scrum-glossary), Agile Alliance glossary (https://agilealliance.org/agile101/agile-glossary/), NN/g Lean UX & Agile glossary (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/agile-glossary/).

Confirmed, recurring terms with working definitions (paraphrased from the search results, cross-referenced across 3+ of the above sources):
- **User story** — smallest unit of Agile work, written from the user's perspective, focused on business value.
- **Backlog refinement** ("grooming") — ongoing activity where the Product Owner and team add detail, estimates, and order to backlog items.
- **Definition of Done (DoD)** — an agreed checklist an increment must satisfy before being called complete; formally part of the official Scrum Guide.
- **Spike** — time-boxed research work (technical or user-focused) to reduce uncertainty, distinct from a Definition of Ready which is a team-adopted practice **not** in the official Scrum Guide.
- **Sprint** — a fixed-length iteration (commonly 1–4 weeks; one source said 2–6, which is longer than the Scrum Guide's usual 1-month cap — **minor inconsistency across sources, note as such**).

These are safe to include as a bounded, well-documented set — multiple independent glossaries agree on definitions with no contested numbers here (unlike Section 1).

## 4. Fintech / payments domain terminology

### 4.1 Card payments value chain — authoritative source found

The single best authoritative, regulator-grade source located: **Bank for International Settlements, Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures, "A Glossary of Terms Used in Payments and Settlement Systems"** — https://www.bis.org/cpmi/glossary_030301.pdf (original 2003 edition) and the actively maintained online version https://www.bis.org/cpmi/publ/d00b.htm. This is a central-bank-consortium-authored glossary, the strongest possible source class for this domain, and it is queryable term-by-term online (each term has its own permalink, e.g. https://www.bis.org/cpmi/publ/d00b.htm?selection=30 for "Final settlement").

Terms confirmed present and defined by industry-glossary cross-referencing (Worldpay https://platforms.worldpay.com/blog/glossary-117-software-led-payments-terms-to-know/, Swipesum https://www.swipesum.com/insights/a-guide-to-payment-terminology-glossary-of-terms, Razorpay https://razorpay.com/blog/payment-processing-terms-glossary-merchants/):
- **Issuing** — the card issuer (bank) that provides the card to the consumer.
- **Acquiring** — the acquiring bank, licensed by card networks, that enables merchants to accept card payments.
- **Authorisation** — the merchant's request, at time of sale, for the issuer to confirm funds/credit availability.
- **Capture** — converting an authorised transaction into one ready for settlement (distinct step from authorisation, confirmed across sources but exact technical definition not independently re-verified from a primary source in this session).
- **Settlement** — the acquiring bank transferring actual funds to the merchant, typically **1–3 days** after the sale (Swipesum/industry-glossary consensus figure).
- **Clearing** — the interchange network (Visa/Mastercard/Amex/Discover) process of exchanging transaction data between issuers and acquirers before settlement.
- **Interchange** — the fee/system for passing fees between issuer and acquirer within the card network's domestic/international scheme.
- **Chargeback** — a card-issuer-initiated reversal demand against a merchant for a disputed/fraudulent transaction.
- **Dispute** — the broader consumer/merchant process that can lead to a chargeback (used in industry glossaries as the parent term to chargeback).
- **Three-Domain Secure (3-D Secure / 3DS)** — confirmed via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-D_Secure and PCI Security Standards Council FAQ (https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org/documents/FAQs_for_PCI_3DS_Core_Security_Standard.pdf): named for its **three domains** — (1) acquirer domain (merchant + acquiring bank), (2) issuer domain (card issuer + access control server), (3) interoperability domain (the card network supporting 3DS).
- **Tokenisation** — per PCI Security Standards Council's Token Service Provider (TSP) Standard, tokenisation is performed by Token Service Providers generating/issuing **EMV payment tokens**, under the EMV Payment Tokenisation Specification Technical Framework (https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org/standards/).
- **PCI DSS** — the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard, defining baseline technical/operational requirements for environments that store, process, or transmit payment account data (https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org/standards/).

### 4.2 Core banking / ledger terms

- **Nostro / Vostro** — same account, two names depending on viewpoint: the account-owning bank calls it "nostro" ("ours"); the account-holding correspondent bank calls the identical account "vostro" ("yours"). Foundational to correspondent banking (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nostro_and_vostro_accounts, https://www.spark.money/glossary/nostro-vostro).
- **Reconciliation** — nostro reconciliation specifically = matching a bank's internal FX records against correspondent bank statements, commonly received via **SWIFT MT940/MT950 messages** (https://www.skydo.com/blog/nostro-reconciliation).
- **Float / funds availability / pre-funding** — a bank must hold sufficient balance in its nostro account before it can process an outbound payment in that currency — this "pre-funding" requirement is the standard explanation for float in correspondent banking (same source as above).
- **Ledger / posting** — general ledger and accounting modules in core banking software record nostro/vostro balances as book entries on each side (https://advapay.eu/understanding-the-general-ledger-and-accounting-module-in-core-banking-software/).
- The **BIS CPMI glossary** (4.1 above) is also the authoritative source for settlement-related ledger terms (final settlement, settlement obligation, settlement asset, deferred net settlement) — confirmed present via individual permalinked entries at bis.org/cpmi/publ/d00b.htm.

### 4.3 Regulatory / compliance terms

- **KYC (Know Your Customer)** — identity-verification process; part of the broader KYC/AML compliance framework (https://kycaid.com/glossary/, https://www.lseg.com/en/risk-intelligence/glossary/kyc/kyc-screening).
- **AML (Anti-Money Laundering)** — broader financial-crime detection (fraud, bribery, money laundering, terrorism financing), distinct from sanctions screening, which specifically checks for sanctioned parties/activities (https://www.lseg.com/en/risk-intelligence/glossary/sanctions/sanctions-screening).
- **PSD2** (EU Payment Services Directive 2) — introduced **Strong Customer Authentication (SCA)** and mandated **open banking** access. Confirmed via multiple sources including PA Consulting (https://www.paconsulting.com/insights/psd2-lets-open-up-about-anti-money-laundering-and-open-banking).
- **SCA — official regulatory definition**, confirmed from EBA-mandated Regulatory Technical Standards (Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2018/389, amended by (EU) 2022/2360): "authentication using at least two elements in the categories of **knowledge** (something only the user knows), **possession** (something only the user possesses) or **inherence** (something the user is), which are independent of each other" — plus, for remote transactions, elements that **dynamically link the transaction to a specific amount and payee** (https://www.eba.europa.eu, corroborated by https://www.corbado.com/blog/psd2-sca-requirements). This is a precise, regulator-sourced definition — high confidence.
- **Open Banking** — the UK-specific implementation of PSD2's third-party access mandate (account access, payment initiation) — confirmed general concept, not independently re-verified against the UK Open Banking Implementation Entity's own glossary in this session.

### 4.4 Modern fintech infrastructure terms

- **ISO 20022** — confirmed via SWIFT's own site (https://www.swift.com/news-events/news/iso-20022-new-era-global-payments): SWIFT acts as the **Registration Authority** for ISO 20022; the **coexistence period between MT and ISO 20022 messages for cross-border payments (CBPR+) ended 22 November 2025**, marking full migration (recent, confirmed current as of report date — not stale). ISO 20022 is described as the shared structured-data messaging standard now underlying **SEPA, SWIFT, FedWire, RTP, and FedNow** (https://www.jpmorgan.com/insights/treasury/treasury-management/what-is-iso-20022, https://www.usbank.com/corporate-and-commercial-banking/insights/payments-hub/trends/demystifying-iso-20022.html).
- **SEPA / SWIFT / Faster Payments** — all confirmed as real, distinct payment rails/schemes actively migrating to ISO 20022 messaging (same sources as above).
- **Payment rails** — used informally across the sources as the umbrella term for the underlying transport/clearing infrastructure (SEPA, SWIFT, Faster Payments, FedNow, RTP) — confirmed as standard industry usage, not from one single formal glossary entry.
- **Virtual accounts** — **no dedicated authoritative source verified in this session**; only found in passing industry-blog mentions, not in a regulator/central-bank glossary. Flag as: term is real and commonly used in the field, but I did not verify a formal definition against an authoritative source — treat definition as needing confirmation before teaching it precisely.
- **Embedded finance** — confirmed general definition ("integration of financial services — payments, lending, insurance, savings — into non-financial products/platforms"), sourced from industry glossaries (Fourthline, TreviPay, OpenBankingTracker) — **no BIS/CFPB/World Bank primary regulatory definition found** despite a targeted search; flag as industry-consensus term, not yet formally codified by a regulator in the sources checked.
- **BNPL (Buy Now, Pay Later)**:
  - **CFPB** (https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_buy-now-pay-later-market-trends-consumer-impacts_report_2022-09.pdf, https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-buy-now-pay-later-bnpl-loan-en-2119/): explicitly states **"there is no single definition of BNPL"**; for its own report, CFPB defines it operationally as the **"pay-in-four" / "split pay"** product — a four-installment, interest-free consumer loan, typically 25% down payment plus three further installments at two-week intervals.
  - **BIS** (https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt2312e.pdf, "Buy now, pay later: a cross-country analysis"): confirms the interest-free-installment framing and adds that BNPL loans are **typically not reported to credit bureaus**, with merchants paying fees to shift credit risk to BNPL platforms.
  - This is a rare case where the regulator itself flags the term as **contested/undefined** — worth teaching the learner that "BNPL" has no single canonical definition, only an operational one per report.

## RECOMMENDED LAYER ORDER

Grounded strictly in the findings above — each band cites the specific evidence that justifies its position in the sequence.

1. **General high-frequency core, ~2,000–3,000 word families (NGSL-style band)** — justification: this is the only band with converging evidence across every source in Section 1 as the *first* meaningful milestone; even the more pessimistic Adolphs & Schmitt (2003) estimate treats 2,000–3,000 as the starting reference point before showing it under-covers spoken discourse, and van Zeeland & Schmitt (2012) accept it as sufficient for a workable (if not perfect) 95% listening-coverage threshold. Nothing later in the sequence is usable if this band is missing.

2. **Formulaic multi-word units / collocations layered onto the same 2,000–3,000 word core, not after it** — justification: Erman & Warren (2000) found formulaic sequences make up 58.6% of spoken discourse, and Boers et al. (2006) found formulaic-sequence usage correlates with native-judge fluency ratings (rho ≈ .44) independent of vocabulary size. This is not a separate later stage — it is a different *unit of learning* applied to the same frequency band from day one, matching this project's existing design decision (card unit = collocation, not word).

3. **Extended general core, ~5,000–6,000 word families** — justification: closes the gap between the contested lower bound (2,000–3,000, Section 1.2) and the more conservative, better-evidenced comfortable-spoken-comprehension threshold (Adolphs & Schmitt's ~5,000; Nation's 6,000–7,000 for unassisted spoken comprehension). This is where "free conversation on everyday topics" becomes realistic rather than merely survivable.

4. **Business Service List, ~1,700 words (Browne & Culligan)** — justification: documented 97% coverage of general business text when combined with the general-English base (Section 2.1), and it is the only evidence-backed specialised list available at the "general professional register" level — it should load before the narrower fintech/BA layers because it is the register those narrower layers sit inside (a BA writes business documents, not just domain jargon).

5. **BA / Systems Analyst + Agile terminology (BABOK + IREB core terms + Scrum/agile glossary set, Sections 3.1–3.3)** — justification: this is the daily-use professional vocabulary confirmed by three independent, standards-body-maintained glossaries (IIBA, IREB, Scrum Guide/Alliance/Agile Alliance) with no contested numbers — a bounded, well-documented, non-controversial set that should load once general professional register (layer 4) is in place, since BA terms are frequently used *inside* business-register sentences.

6. **Fintech/payments core (Section 4.1–4.3): issuing, acquiring, authorisation, capture, settlement, clearing, interchange, chargeback/dispute, 3DS, tokenisation, PCI DSS, nostro/vostro, KYC/AML, PSD2/SCA/open banking** — justification: this is the domain-specific layer with the strongest authoritative sourcing found in this whole report (BIS CPMI glossary, PCI Security Standards Council, EBA regulatory technical standards) — it should load last among the "solid" layers because it presupposes both general professional vocabulary (layer 4) and BA process vocabulary (layer 5) to be usable in a real work sentence (e.g. "the acceptance criteria for the chargeback dispute flow require SCA re-authentication").

7. **Modern fintech infrastructure terms with weaker/contested sourcing (ISO 20022, SEPA/SWIFT/Faster Payments rails — solid; virtual accounts, embedded finance, BNPL — flagged as loosely or inconsistently defined even by regulators)** — justification: placed last, deliberately, because the evidence itself is weaker here. ISO 20022/SEPA/SWIFT are solidly sourced (SWIFT, JPMorgan, US Bank) and could arguably sit inside layer 6, but embedded finance has no regulator definition found, virtual accounts has no authoritative source verified at all, and BNPL is explicitly flagged by the CFPB itself as having "no single definition." Teaching these last, and teaching them *as contested/evolving terms* rather than fixed facts, is the honest response to what the evidence shows.

**IT-specific technical word lists (Section 2.5) are deliberately NOT given their own layer** — the evidence found (NCSAWL, CS Academic Vocabulary List) targets CS *research-paper* register, not BA/fintech working vocabulary, and no published list matches this learner's actual professional need. That gap should be filled by curated glossary terms from Sections 3–4, not by importing an ill-fitting academic word list.

## Gaps and limitations (stated explicitly, per fact-checking discipline)

- **BABOK and IREB exact term definitions were not extracted** — both official sources exist and are correctly identified, but content was gated (IIBA membership paywall) or unparseable (IREB PDF encoding/certificate errors). What is reported for these two is the confirmed *existence and structure* of the terms, not verified exact wording. This should be re-attempted with a member login or a plain-text PDF extraction tool if exact BABOK/IREB wording is needed for card content.
- **"2,000 words = 80% of everyday conversation"** — could not be traced to one primary academic source; treat as a popularised approximation, not a citable number.
- **"8,000–10,000 word families for genuine fluency"** — sourced only from non-academic aggregator sites; do not treat as evidence-backed.
- **Virtual accounts, embedded finance** — no authoritative regulator/central-bank definition found; only industry-blog sourcing.
- **BNPL** — CFPB itself states there is no single definition; report this to the learner as a genuinely contested term, not settled fact.
- **No evidence found** of a published word list specifically for "IT Business Analyst" or "fintech Business Analyst" register — this vocabulary must be hand-curated from Sections 3–4, not sourced from a frequency list.
- Written-vs-spoken coverage percentage table in 1.1 is flagged as directionally correct but not confidently attributed to one paper.
